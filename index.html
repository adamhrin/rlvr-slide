Reinforcement Learning (RL)
thinks step-by-step, checks its own work
If 3x + 7 = 22, what is x?
Let me solve step by step. 3x + 7 = 22 → subtract 7: 3x = 15 → divide by 3: x = 5 Check: 3(5) + 7 = 22 ✓ Answer: x = 5
Nobody taught it to "let me check" — RL kept rewarding rollouts that did.

GRPO: take the average reward of the group as a baseline. Each rollout's advantage is how much it beat (or lost to) that average. No critic network needed. Step 3: push πθ toward chosen, away from rejected
The loss raises the log-probability of the chosen answer and lowers the log-probability of the rejected answer — relative to the frozen πref. No environment, no rollouts, no separate reward model. Hover any token above to see its top-5 prediction shift.
$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\log \sigma\!\left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)} \right) $$
DPO collapses the whole RL loop into a supervised contrastive objective. Cheaper, more stable — but you can't react to the model's own evolving outputs and you need a curated preference dataset.
⚠ A subtle property of DPO: the per-token shading is uniform (no credit assignment), and the gradient magnitude shrinks as the model already prefers chosen — examples it gets "right" stop contributing to learning.

the takeaway

The difficulty of writing the verifier IS the difficulty of formalizing the goal.

Formulate your task in a way that's easily verifiable — and you're well-equipped not just to train a model on it, but to use one effectively.

The same clarity that makes RL work makes any AI system work.

The loop is easy. The data and the scale are everything.
