⛓ KL leash
At every update we also add a penalty β·KL(πθ ∥ πref) — πref is a frozen copy (usually the SFT init). Without it, the model can drift into degenerate strategies that game the reward but produce gibberish. $$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}^{\text{clip}} - \beta\,\text{KL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}})$$
✂ Clipping
The actual loss uses an importance ratio ρ = πθ(token) / πθ_old(token), clipped to [1−ε, 1+ε] (ε≈0.2). This prevents a single update from changing token probabilities by more than ~20%, which keeps training stable. $$\mathcal{L}^{\text{clip}} = \mathbb{E}[\min(\rho A,\;\text{clip}(\rho,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A)]$$
⚖ GRPO vs PPO
PPO trains a second network — a critic Vφ — to predict expected reward, and uses that as the baseline. GRPO drops the critic entirely and uses the group's mean reward instead. Cheaper, simpler, surprisingly competitive. The trade-off: PPO can give per-token (per-state) baselines via GAE; GRPO's baseline is per-prompt only.
⚠ Per-token caveat
In GRPO every token in a rollout gets the same advantage signal (uniform shading above). This is the credit-assignment problem: a "bad" rollout penalizes all its tokens equally, even ones that were fine in isolation. Process reward models (PRMs) try to fix this by scoring intermediate steps — but they're hard to build well.

The loss raises the log-probability of the chosen answer and lowers the log-probability of the rejected answer — relative to the frozen πref. No environment, no rollouts, no separate reward model.
$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\log \sigma\!\left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)} \right) $$
DPO collapses the whole RL loop into a supervised contrastive objective. Cheaper, more stable — but you can't react to the model's own evolving outputs and you need a curated preference dataset.

The punchline
Each loop nudges the model by a tiny amount. Done a few hundred thousand times — across thousands of math, code, and logic problems — those nudges compound into the long, self-correcting chains of reasoning we see in models like DeepSeek-R1, Qwen-3 reasoning, GPT-o-series, and Claude's extended thinking. No-one explicitly taught the model to say "wait, let me reconsider." The verifier just kept rewarding rollouts that arrived at the right answer, and the model figured out that going slow and checking its work was the strategy with the highest expected reward.

…and yet, what we don't know

The mechanics above are well understood. The deeper questions about why this works as well as it does, and where it breaks, are very much open. Honest version of the slide:

Open question 1
Does RL teach reasoning, or just elicit it?
On many benchmarks, pass@1 improves dramatically after RL — but pass@k at large k often doesn't. That suggests RL is sharpening behaviors the base model could already produce sometimes, not adding fundamentally new skills. We don't yet have a clean test that settles it either way.
Open question 2
Reward hacking is real and weird
Models discover shortcuts: padding answers with reasoning theater that doesn't change the conclusion, gaming format checks, exploiting bugs in the verifier, or — with LLM judges — learning what the judge likes rather than what's actually correct. The KL leash helps; it doesn't fix it.
Open question 3
Most of what we want isn't verifiable
"Was this email helpful? Was this story good? Was that answer safe?" — there's no \boxed{} to check. For those domains we fall back to preference learning (RLHF, DPO) or generative reward models (an LLM scoring against a rubric). Whether the reasoning gains from verifiable RL transfer to these messier domains is an active area of research.
The takeaway
A simple loop — generate a few attempts, score them with a checker, nudge the model toward the better ones — is the engine behind a lot of the reasoning ability of today's frontier models. It's not magic; the math fits on one slide. But it works astonishingly well at scale, and the open-source community is still mapping out exactly why and exactly where.
